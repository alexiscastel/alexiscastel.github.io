For years, I had not paid much attention to my electricity supplier. I had selected Energy Harbor and, at some point, asked for carbon-free electricity. Then the original contract expired. I was automatically enrolled in a new, more expensive contract, and I did not treat it as urgent enough to revisit.

My July 2026 bill finally gave me a reason to look carefully. Energy Harbor was charging 11.59 cents per kWh under an 18-month contract. During that billing period, we used 881 kWh, so the supply portion alone was $102.11. Over the previous twelve months, our home had used 12,522 kWh.

The rate was not catastrophic, but it was higher than several alternatives. More importantly, I was no longer confident that the product still matched the environmental choice I thought I had made years earlier.

So I went shopping for electricity in Ohio.

## My initial criteria

I started with Ohio's [Apples to Apples comparison site][1] and applied a few basic filters:

- I wanted 100% renewable or genuinely carbon-free electricity.
- I preferred a contract or program lasting at least 24 months.
- I wanted a straightforward price per kWh.
- I rejected short introductory terms, complicated rewards, and cash-back offers that made the real price harder to understand.
- I wanted either no cancellation fee or a very good reason to accept one.
- I did not want to pay a large premium merely for green marketing.

After removing the obviously unattractive offers, I was left with a manageable group: AEP Energy, Brighten, CleanChoice, CleanSky, Energy Harbor, Inspire, IGS, and eventually NOPEC.

That was when the research became more interesting.

## "100% renewable" does not mean renewable electricity is routed to my house

Electricity on the grid cannot be directed from a particular wind turbine to a particular home. Most retail green-energy products instead use **Renewable Energy Certificates**, or RECs.

One REC represents the environmental attributes associated with one megawatt-hour of renewable electricity generated and placed on the grid. A supplier can sell me ordinary grid electricity while purchasing and retiring enough RECs to match my consumption.

That mechanism is legitimate, but the words **"100% renewable" do not tell the whole story**.

The questions that matter include:

- What kind of renewable generation produced the RECs?
- Where and when were they generated?
- Which registry tracks them?
- Are they retired so nobody else can claim them?
- Is the product independently certified?
- Does the purchase merely buy the cheapest available credits, or does it meaningfully support renewable development?

This is where the contracts differed.

## The offers looked similar until I read the details

The following rates were the offers I reviewed in early August 2026. They are a snapshot, not permanent recommendations.

| Supplier and product | Rate | Initial term | Exit fee | Environmental claim | Independent certification promised |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| CleanSky Embrace Green | 10.19¢/kWh | 24 months | $150 | RECs matching 100% of use | No; the contract explicitly allowed non-certified RECs |
| Energy Harbor Carbon Free | 10.49¢/kWh | 36 months | $0 | Certificates from nuclear, wind, solar, or hydro | No named product certification |
| Brighten | 10.59¢/kWh | 24 months | $0 | RECs matching 100% of use | Not promised; the contract said attributes might or might not be certified |
| CleanChoice | 10.70¢/kWh | 24 months | $0 | Wind and solar RECs | Not Green-e certified |
| Inspire | 10.79¢/kWh | 24 months | $0 | Wind, solar, or low-impact hydro RECs | Not Green-e certified |
| AEP ECO-Advantage | 11.45¢/kWh | 36 months | $0 | 100% national wind RECs | **Green-e Energy certified** |
| IGS | 13.99¢/kWh | 24 months | $75 | Green offer | Too expensive to remain competitive in my comparison |

A few things stood out.

### CleanSky was cheap, but the contract was weak

CleanSky offered the lowest rate among my finalists, but it also charged a $150 early termination fee. More importantly, its terms explicitly said that it could use **non-certified RECs** from a broad range of sources.

That does not make the product fraudulent. It means I would be relying primarily on the supplier's contractual promise, without the additional assurance of a named independent certification.

### Brighten, CleanChoice, and Inspire were reasonable—but not certified

These offers were not obvious scams. Each promised to match my usage with RECs, and some provided more information than others about the resource mix.

However, none of the Ohio products I reviewed promised Green-e certification. Brighten's contract was particularly revealing: it said the environmental attributes associated with its RECs **may, but need not, be verified or certified**.

The rates were attractive, but I would be buying a green claim with less independent assurance.

### AEP had the cleanest retail contract

AEP's ECO-Advantage plan was the strongest conventional retail offer from an environmental-verification standpoint. It matched 100% of usage with wind RECs, was explicitly **Green-e Energy certified**, and included annual product-content reporting.

It was also more expensive: 11.45 cents per kWh. That was still slightly below my existing Energy Harbor rate, but roughly one cent per kWh above some uncertified alternatives.

AEP showed me what a well-documented green product looks like. The remaining question was whether I could get comparable assurance at a better price.

### Energy Harbor's carbon-free offer was a legitimate alternative

Energy Harbor offered a new Carbon Free plan at 10.49 cents per kWh. It matched usage with emission-free certificates sourced from nuclear, wind, solar, or hydro generation.

This was not a renewable-only product because nuclear energy was included, but it was a serious low-carbon option. I do not consider nuclear power disqualifying; keeping existing nuclear generation online can be valuable for reducing power-sector emissions.

Still, the contract did not guarantee a particular resource mix or name an independent certification for the product. It was a good carbon-focused value, but not the strongest answer to my specific desire for certified renewable energy.

## The option I almost missed: NOPEC

Lakewood belongs to the Northeast Ohio Public Energy Council, or [NOPEC][2]. NOPEC is a governmental energy aggregator that negotiates electricity programs for participating Ohio communities.

It did not appear alongside ordinary retail offers in the way I expected on Apples to Apples, so I initially overlooked it.

Lakewood participates in NOPEC's **Green Community Choice** program. The program matches 100% of participating customers' usage with **Green-e-certified renewable energy credits**, using nationally located renewable resources such as wind or solar. The agreement also has no early termination fee.

Beginning with September 2026 meter reads, the published Green Community Choice rate for The Illuminating Company territory was **10.599 cents per kWh**.

At our recent annual usage of 12,522 kWh, that is approximately:

- **$1,327 per year** for the supply portion at the published NOPEC rate;
- about **$124 per year less** than continuing at Energy Harbor's 11.59-cent rate; and
- roughly **$108 per year less** than AEP's 11.45-cent Green-e-certified offer.

The savings are not life-changing. The appeal is that NOPEC combines a competitive rate, independently certified renewable attributes, no cancellation fee, and a community aggregation structure.

## An important caveat: Green Community Choice is not a fixed-rate plan

This was another example of why the exact contract matters.

The Green Community Choice agreement runs through June 2029, but the **price is not fixed for the entire term**. NOPEC's terms allow the program price to change after the stated rate period, generally with the new price published in advance. The program has no early termination fee, so I can leave if the pricing stops being competitive.

NOPEC also offers a 24-month fixed product with 100% renewable content. In September 2026, that option was listed at 10.949 cents per kWh—about $44 more per year for our household than the Green Community Choice rate.

I decided that the lower Green Community rate and freedom to leave were worth accepting some rate variability. Someone who values complete price certainty might reasonably choose the 24-month fixed renewable option instead.

## My decision

I am switching from Energy Harbor to **NOPEC Green Community Choice**.

It is the best overall fit for what I was actually trying to buy:

- renewable attributes matched to 100% of our electricity use;
- Green-e-certified RECs rather than an undefined or explicitly non-certified product;
- a rate below both my current supplier and the utility's current comparison rate;
- no early termination fee; and
- enough transparency to understand what I am—and am not—purchasing.

I am not claiming that buying RECs is the same as installing solar panels, signing a long-term power-purchase agreement with a new wind farm, or directly causing a particular renewable project to be built. It is still an accounting mechanism layered onto ordinary grid electricity.

But accounting mechanisms can be either weak or credible. Certification, tracking, retirement, and clear disclosures matter.

## What I learned

The most useful lesson was not that one supplier is universally best. Rates and offers change. The lesson was that a green label is only the beginning of the comparison.

When evaluating a green electricity offer, I would now check:

1. **The actual price and contract term**
2. **Monthly and early-termination fees**
3. **What happens automatically when the term expires**
4. **Whether 100% of usage is matched**
5. **Which resources qualify**
6. **Whether the RECs are independently certified**
7. **Whether the rate itself is fixed or variable**
8. **Whether the supplier identifies the registry and retirement process**

My own inattention allowed an old contract to roll into a more expensive one. Correcting it should save a modest amount of money, but the more important result is that I now understand what environmental product I am paying for.

The devil was not in the headline rate. It was in the RECs.
